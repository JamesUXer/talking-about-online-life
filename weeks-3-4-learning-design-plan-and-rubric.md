# Weeks 3 and 4: detailed learning design plan and rubric

**Project:** Talking About Online Life
**Audience:** All primary school staff
**Status:** Final planning draft, ready for safeguarding and implementation review
**Prepared:** 26 August 2026
**Target format:** Two short, connected web modules within the existing four-week static site

## Recommendation

Build Weeks 3 and 4 as two linked pages in the existing site, not as a separate React/Vite application.

The current workspace contains static HTML pages for Weeks 1 and 2, shared CSS and shared JavaScript. It has no `package.json`, `src` directory or Vite configuration. Adding a second technical stack would create two versions of the same product and make maintenance harder. If a React/Vite migration is still wanted, that should be a separate, explicit piece of work.

The learning sequence should be:

**Week 1: notice online life → Week 2: make conversation normal → Week 3: choose a proportionate response → Week 4: repeat one role-relevant habit**

This gives Weeks 3 and 4 distinct jobs. Week 3 develops judgement in an ordinary moment. Week 4 turns that judgement into a shared practice across the school.

## The design problem

Staff do not mainly need more facts about platforms. They need to recognise that a passing comment can matter, feel able to ask one calm question, and know when an everyday conversation has become a safeguarding concern.

The wider culture shift is from:

> Online safety belongs to the computing lead, DSL, senior leaders or IT support.

to:

> Any trusted adult can notice, listen and pass on a concern. Specialist staff still lead the safeguarding response.

The module must hold both parts together. Curiosity helps open the door. It does not replace professional judgement, recording or the school’s safeguarding procedure.

## Audience and use context

The experience is for teachers, teaching assistants, support staff, lunchtime and playground staff, office and admin staff, leaders, volunteers and other trusted adults.

Design assumptions to validate in the pilot:

- Staff may complete a week on a phone, tablet or staff computer.
- They may have five to ten minutes and be interrupted.
- Confidence with technology and online culture will vary.
- Some staff will have strong safeguarding knowledge but little confidence discussing games, apps or online friendships.
- Some staff will not work in a classroom, so classroom-only examples would weaken the whole-school message.
- The tone must reduce pressure without making the safeguarding boundary vague.

## Behavioural aim and learning outcomes

### Overall behavioural aim

Staff feel more able to notice, ask, respond and escalate when online life appears in an ordinary school moment.

### Week 3 outcome

Given a short pupil comment, the learner can choose a calm, proportionate next response and explain when to move from curiosity to the school’s safeguarding procedure.

At the end of Week 3, the learner should be able to:

1. recognise a natural opening without needing platform knowledge;
2. select one open, non-blaming follow-up question;
3. avoid interrogating the child or asking to inspect a device as a default response;
4. identify when a concern should be reported through the school’s normal route.

### Week 4 outcome

Given their role or closest working context, the learner can choose one small online-life conversation habit, connect it to a realistic cue in their week and state how they will pass on a concern.

At the end of Week 4, the learner should be able to:

1. describe how their role contributes to a whole-school conversation culture;
2. choose one repeatable action within the boundaries of that role;
3. create and export a short action plan;
4. retain the distinction between noticing a pattern and investigating a concern.

### What the module is not trying to do

- teach staff the features, age ratings or risks of every platform;
- test recall of policy wording;
- train staff to investigate a disclosure;
- collect child-related examples or personal reflections;
- imply that a conversation alone resolves a safeguarding concern;
- measure behaviour change through page views or button clicks.

## Learning progression across the four weeks

| Week | Learner question | New behaviour | Evidence inside the experience |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Online life is everyday life | Why does this belong in my day? | Notice online life as part of ordinary childhood. | Learner identifies what an adult could notice in one comment. |
| 2. Make online life normal to talk about | Why talk before a problem appears? | Use regular, low-pressure conversation to build trust. | Learner selects a repeatable opener. |
| 3. Spotting natural openings | What could I say in this moment? | Choose a calm, proportionate follow-up. | Learner responds to varied everyday scenarios and chooses one phrase to try. |
| 4. A whole-school habit | How does this become part of our culture? | Repeat one role-relevant habit and pass on concerns appropriately. | Learner chooses a role-based habit, completes a safeguarding checkpoint and exports an action plan. |

## Experience structure

Use two linked pages to preserve the current navigation pattern and keep each week short.

### Week 3 journey, about six minutes

1. Bridge from Week 2
2. One-minute explanation of natural openings
3. “Is this a natural opening?” scenario activity
4. Short debrief and safeguarding boundary
5. Choose one phrase to try
6. Continue to Week 4

### Week 4 journey, about six to seven minutes

1. Bridge from individual response to shared culture
2. Choose the role or context closest to the learner’s day
3. Choose one small habit
4. Complete one safeguarding checkpoint
5. Build “My small action”
6. Copy, download or open the plan in the learner’s own email app

The time estimates are design targets, not measured completion times. Pilot testing should record actual time, interruption and abandonment.

## Week 3 detailed plan: spotting natural openings

### Purpose

Help staff see that online safety conversations do not need to be formal, serious or scheduled. The professional skill is to notice the moment, use one calm question and listen to what comes next.

### Main message

> Small moments can become safe conversations.

### Staff shift

From:

> I need to know exactly what to say before I ask.

Towards:

> I can use simple curiosity to open the door.

### Section 1: opening and bridge

**Eyebrow:** Week 3 of 4
**Heading:** Spotting natural openings
**Lead:** Children often mention online life in passing. You do not need a script or expert knowledge of the platform. One calm question can help you understand what the moment means to them.
**Core message:** Small moments can become safe conversations.
**Primary action:** Try the moments

Supporting copy:

> Week 2 focused on making online life normal to talk about. This week is about what to do when an opening appears. The aim is not to turn every comment into a lesson. Notice the moment, ask one useful question and listen.

### Section 2: the response pattern

Present one compact pattern before the activity:

1. **Notice** the online-life cue.
2. **Ask** one calm, open question.
3. **Listen** without blame or surprise.
4. **Act** through the school’s safeguarding procedure if a concern appears.

This is guidance, not a mnemonic staff must memorise. The verbs should help learners understand the sequence without adding a test.

Include this pressure-reducing line:

> You are opening a conversation, not trying to solve the whole situation on the spot.

### Section 3: interactive activity

**Activity title:** Is this a natural opening?
**Instruction:** Read each moment and choose the response that best keeps the door open.
**Progress:** Moment 1 of 5, shown in text as well as visually
**Interaction:** One scenario at a time, with three real buttons and immediate feedback
**Completion:** Learners may change an answer and revisit a previous moment. Nothing is locked behind a perfect score.

The original yes/no idea has been strengthened. All five examples could become openings, so a simple “yes” on every card would not show judgement. The final activity asks which response is proportionate to the context.

#### Moment 1: late gaming

**What the adult hears**

> “I was up really late playing last night.”

**Prompt:** What would you say first?

| Option | Response | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A, strongest | “What were you playing?” | This opens the conversation without assuming that the game is the problem. Listen to what the child says next. Tiredness may be an ordinary one-off or part of a wider pattern. |
| B | “You should not be gaming that late.” | This moves quickly to judgement and may close the conversation. Understanding what happened gives you more useful context. |
| C | Say nothing because it happened at home. | Online experiences can affect sleep, mood, friendships and learning in school. A brief follow-up is reasonable even when the activity happened elsewhere. |

**Learning point:** An everyday comment can be enough to ask one neutral question.

#### Moment 2: a “weird but funny” video

**What the adult hears**

> “That video was weird but funny.”

**Prompt:** Which response keeps the tone open?

| Option | Response | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A, strongest | “What made it weird?” | This gently explores the child’s reaction. It does not suggest that they have done something wrong. |
| B | “Do not watch strange videos.” | The adult still does not know what the child saw or how it affected them. A warning at this point may replace useful listening. |
| C | “Show me the video now.” | Asking to inspect content can increase pressure and is not the default first step. Start with the child’s account. If a safeguarding concern emerges, follow the school’s procedure. |

**Learning point:** Ask about meaning and feeling before making a judgement about the content.

#### Moment 3: blocking someone

**What the adult hears**

> “I blocked someone yesterday.”

**Prompt:** What is the most useful first response?

| Option | Response | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A, strongest | “What happened?” | Blocking may have been a sensible action, but the comment could also point to conflict, pressure or unwanted contact. Ask once, listen and follow the school’s safeguarding route if anything feels concerning. |
| B | “Good, so it is sorted.” | Blocking may not be the end of the situation. This response could close the conversation before the child explains why they acted. |
| C | “Give me the username and I will look into it.” | Staff should not begin their own online investigation. Listen to the child and use the school’s established reporting process. |

**Learning point:** A sensible action by the child does not always mean that no further support is needed.

#### Moment 4: a new online friend

**What the adult hears**

> “I made a new friend in my game.”

**Prompt:** Which response shows calm curiosity?

| Option | Response | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A, strongest | “How did you get to know them?” | This invites context without immediately treating the friendship as suspicious. What the child says next may reassure you or suggest a concern that needs passing on. |
| B | “People online are not real friends.” | This dismisses something that may matter to the child and could make them less likely to talk about it again. |
| C | “What is their real name and where do they live?” | This becomes an investigation and asks for information the child may not know. Begin with the child’s experience and follow safeguarding procedures if needed. |

**Learning point:** Balance interest with safeguarding awareness. Avoid either alarm or false reassurance.

#### Moment 5: a private game chat

**Context:** Other pupils are nearby.
**What the adult hears**

> “I do not want to show you my game chat. It is private.”

**Prompt:** What would be the most proportionate response?

| Option | Response | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A | “If it is safe, you should be able to show me.” | Children can have a reasonable wish for privacy. Pressure may close the conversation and could expose private material unnecessarily. |
| B, strongest | “You do not need to show me. Is anything in the chat making you worried or uncomfortable?” | This respects privacy while leaving a safe opening. If the child wants to talk, move to an appropriate setting, listen and follow the school’s safeguarding procedure if a concern emerges. |
| C | “All right, forget I asked.” | Respecting privacy does not require closing the door. One low-pressure check gives the child a chance to say whether something is wrong. |

**Learning point:** Privacy and safety are not opposites. Do not make showing a device the price of being listened to.

### Activity feedback design

- Show feedback immediately below the selected response.
- Begin with what the response would do to the conversation, then explain the professional consequence.
- Do not use “correct” and “wrong” as the only feedback.
- Keep the child’s likely experience visible: respected, judged, pressured or able to continue.
- Leave the selected response visible and allow another choice.
- Announce the feedback through an `aria-live="polite"` region without moving focus unexpectedly.
- Do not collect or transmit individual answers. Aggregate completion events may record that a moment was attempted, not which answer a named person chose.

### Section 4: debrief and conversation openers

**Heading:** One question is enough to begin

Copy:

> The useful responses did three things: they stayed calm, asked for context and left room for the child to decide what to say next. You do not need to interrogate the child or solve the situation yourself.

Present the openers as selectable phrase cards:

- “What’s that about?”
- “What do you like about it?”
- “Who do you usually play, watch or chat with?”
- “How did that make you feel?”
- “What happened next?”

Add a short note:

> Choose the lightest question that fits the moment. If the child shares a worry, listen carefully and follow your school’s safeguarding procedure.

### Section 5: safeguarding boundary

Use a clearly labelled, calm callout. It should not look like an optional tip.

**Heading:** When the conversation becomes a concern

> Stay calm and listen. Do not promise to keep the information secret and do not begin your own investigation. Follow your school’s child protection policy and report the concern to the DSL or deputy without delay. If a child is in immediate danger, follow the school’s emergency procedure.

The final wording must be checked by the school’s DSL against the school’s own policy before release. The module can reinforce the route but should not replace local induction or policy access.

### Section 6: Week 3 action

**Heading:** Choose one phrase for this week
**Prompt:** Which phrase could you use naturally in your role?
**Interaction:** Select one phrase or write a short alternative.
**Feedback:** “Keep it light. Try the phrase once when an online-life moment appears.”
**Primary action:** Continue to Week 4

Do not ask for a child’s name, a real incident or a description of a concern.

### Week 3 completion criteria

The page is complete when the learner has:

- attempted the five moments;
- seen feedback for each one;
- selected or written one calm opener;
- been shown the safeguarding boundary;
- reached a clear link to Week 4.

Completion should not depend on selecting every preferred response. This is formative professional practice, not a compliance score.

## Week 4 detailed plan: a whole-school habit

### Purpose

Show that online safety culture is created by repeated small actions across many adults. The learner should leave with one action that fits their real role, not a general intention to “talk more”.

### Main message

> When many adults make online-life conversations normal, children are more likely to see school as a safe place to talk.

### Staff shift

From:

> This is something I might do if a concern appears.

Towards:

> This is a small habit we can build together.

### Section 1: opening and bridge

**Eyebrow:** Week 4 of 4
**Heading:** A whole-school habit
**Lead:** Children may speak to any adult they trust. A shared culture grows when different adults notice, listen and pass on concerns through the same safeguarding route.
**Core message:** Small actions become a culture when many adults repeat them.
**Primary action:** Find a habit for my role

Supporting copy:

> Week 3 practised what one adult could say in one moment. Week 4 looks at how those moments add up across classrooms, corridors, the playground, the office and leadership conversations.

### Section 2: role or context selection

**Heading:** Where do you notice children’s online lives?
**Instruction:** Choose the role or context closest to your day. This is only used to suggest habits on this page. It is not sent or stored by the website.
**Options:**

- Classroom teacher
- Teaching assistant or support staff
- Lunchtime or playground staff
- Office or admin staff
- School leader
- Other trusted adult

Use a single-choice `fieldset` with a visible `legend`, or a group of real buttons with accurate selected state. Reveal only the relevant habit panel after selection to keep the page manageable. Provide a “Change role or context” control.

### Section 3: role-based habits

Each option should reveal three small habits. The learner chooses one, not all three.

#### Classroom teacher

1. Ask one neutral follow-up when a child mentions a game, video, app or online friendship.
2. Connect a repeated online theme to an existing PSHE, behaviour or safeguarding conversation.
3. Pass on a repeated or concerning pattern rather than waiting until you feel certain what it means.

#### Teaching assistant or support staff

1. Add one calm online-life question to an ordinary check-in when it fits the conversation.
2. Notice whether a change in mood, friendship or behaviour may have an online connection.
3. Share the child’s words and your concern through the school’s normal safeguarding route rather than investigating it yourself.

#### Lunchtime or playground staff

1. Notice repeated talk about online arguments, exclusion, secrecy or upsetting content.
2. Use one light question, such as “What happened?”, when online conflict surfaces in play.
3. Pass on a concern or repeated pattern to the school’s named safeguarding route, even when the first comment seems small.

#### Office or admin staff

1. Listen for online worries when a parent or carer contacts the school.
2. Capture the concern accurately and pass it to the appropriate safeguarding person in line with school procedure.
3. Use clear, reassuring language: “Thank you for telling us. I’ll pass this to the right person.”

#### School leader

1. Include a brief online-life prompt in regular safeguarding or staff conversations.
2. Give staff a clear route for sharing patterns they are noticing across different parts of school life.
3. Model a calm, child-centred and non-blaming response when online concerns are discussed.

#### Other trusted adult

1. Choose one part of the day when a passing online-life comment is most likely to appear.
2. Keep one calm opener ready, such as “What happened?” or “How did that make you feel?”
3. Check that you know who to contact and how to record or pass on a concern in this school.

### Habit feedback

After a habit is selected, show:

> This works because it is small, tied to a real part of your role and easy to repeat. You are not taking over the DSL’s role. You are making it more likely that a child can reach the school’s safeguarding system through an adult they trust.

Avoid presenting all staff as having the same authority. The action must stay within the selected role while keeping the shared responsibility visible.

### Section 4: scenario checkpoint

The checkpoint tests the boundary between opening a conversation and responding to a safeguarding concern. It should come after role selection so the learner sees that the same boundary applies in every role.

**Heading:** When a small opening becomes a concern

**Scenario**

> A pupil says, “I blocked someone, but they made another account and sent another message.” You ask what happened. The pupil says the person asked for a photo and told them not to tell anyone.

**Prompt:** What should the adult do next?

| Option | Response | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A | Ask to see the messages and search for the account. | Do not begin your own investigation. Preserve the child’s account, stay calm and use the school’s safeguarding process. |
| B, strongest | Reassure the pupil that they were right to tell you, explain that you need to share this with safeguarding staff, and follow the school’s procedure without delay. | This keeps the response child-centred and moves the concern to the people responsible for the safeguarding response. |
| C | Promise not to tell anyone unless it happens again. | Do not promise secrecy or wait for another incident. Explain who needs to know and why, using language the child can understand. |

**Debrief:**

> Curiosity opened the door. Once a safeguarding concern appeared, the adult’s job changed: listen, reassure and follow the school’s procedure.

No score should be shown. If the learner chooses A or C, keep the feedback visible and invite them to choose again. The checkpoint should not block access to the final action, but every learner must be shown the preferred response before completion.

### Section 5: my small action

**Heading:** My small action
**Prompt:** What is one small online safety conversation habit you could repeat this week?

Use the selected role and habit to prefill an editable plan. Include:

1. **My role or context:** selected value, with a change option.
2. **When or where I will try it:** optional short text field, for example “Friday playground duty” or “during morning check-in”.
3. **My small habit:** required editable textarea, prefilled from the selected habit.

Suggested helper text:

> Keep it specific enough to try once. Do not include a child’s name, details of a concern or any confidential information.

Suggested validation message if the action is blank:

> Add one small habit before saving your plan.

### Section 6: export the action plan

Generate the same plain-text summary for all export routes:

```text
Talking About Online Life
My online safety conversation habit

Role or context: [selected role]
When or where: [learner's cue, if supplied]
My small habit: [selected or edited action]

Safeguarding reminder: If a concern emerges, follow the school's child protection procedure and report it through the school's safeguarding route without delay.
```

#### Copy my action

- Use the Clipboard API with the existing fallback pattern.
- Success message: “Copied. You can paste your plan into your own notes.”
- Failure message: “Copy did not work this time. Select the plan text and copy it manually.”
- Announce the result in a polite live region.

#### Download my action

- Create a plain-text `Blob`, use an object URL and revoke it after download.
- File name: `my-online-safety-conversation-habit.txt`.
- Success message: “Your text file has been prepared for download.”
- The visible plan must remain available if the download fails.

#### Email it to myself

- Use `mailto:` with the subject “My online safety conversation habit”.
- Put the plain-text summary in the message body.
- Do not ask for an email address and do not send anything automatically.
- Helper text: “This opens a draft in your own email app. The website does not send the email or store your action plan.”
- If no email app is configured, the copy and download options remain available.

#### Data and privacy rules

- Keep the selected role, cue and action in page memory only.
- Do not write the free text to analytics, local storage, session storage, a server or a URL parameter.
- Do not include child names or incident details in examples entered by a learner.
- If aggregate analytics are retained, record only events such as `week_4_action_export_copy`, not the selected role or action text.
- Explain this at the point of entry and export, not only in a separate privacy page.

### Section 7: completion

**Heading:** One habit, repeated
**Completion copy:**

> You have chosen one action that fits your role. Try it once this week. If an online-life concern appears, listen and use your school’s safeguarding route.

Provide links to return to the four-week overview and revisit Week 3. Do not add an unsupported claim that the learner is now “fully trained” or that completing the page has changed practice.

### Week 4 completion criteria

The page is complete when the learner has:

- chosen a role or context;
- reviewed the habits for that role;
- selected or written one habit;
- seen the preferred response in the safeguarding checkpoint;
- created a non-empty action plan;
- reached the export controls and completion message.

Using an export option should remain optional. The learner may copy the text manually or leave without storing anything.

## Content and visual direction

### Voice

Use the existing copy style: calm, human, curious and non-alarmist. The learner should feel coached, not tested or blamed.

Prefer:

- “notice”, “try”, “one question”, “could”, “when it fits”;
- concrete school moments;
- short feedback that explains the consequence of a response;
- acknowledgement that staff do not need to know every platform.

Avoid:

- “red flags”, “failure to”, “you must know”, “dangerous apps”;
- implying that technology is inherently harmful;
- treating children as risk cases;
- long policy extracts;
- invented quotes presented as research;
- percentages without a relevant, current source and sample context.

### Visual continuity

Reuse the existing annotated-journal system, paper panels, restrained palette, typography and button patterns. Keep the treatment professional. Interaction cards should look like staff CPD prompts rather than children’s quiz cards.

Suggested image direction:

- **Week 3 hero:** a staff notebook page containing three small speech fragments from ordinary school moments, with one calm adult question annotated beside them.
- **Week 4 hero:** a simple school-day map showing classroom, playground, office and leadership contexts connected by repeated speech bubbles.

Do not use platform logos, screenshots of real chats, warning symbols or identifiable children. Decorative imagery should have empty alternative text. Any image that carries learning meaning needs an equivalent text explanation.

### Progress and navigation

- Keep the current header pattern: “Week 3 of 4” and “Week 4 of 4”.
- Update the overview cards from “Coming soon” to active links only when each page is ready.
- Update the Week 2 footer to link to Week 3.
- Week 3 footer: Back to Week 2; Next: Week 4.
- Week 4 footer: Back to Week 3; Return to overview.
- Do not force completion of earlier weeks in the browser. Make the recommended sequence clear while preserving direct access.
- If a visual progress bar is added, include equivalent text and do not imply that page completion equals competence.

## Accessibility and inclusive interaction requirements

The target is WCAG 2.2 AA, supported by manual testing. Automated checks alone are not enough.

### Structure and controls

- Use one `h1`, logical `h2` and `h3` levels, landmarks and a working skip link.
- Use real buttons for actions and links for navigation.
- Use `fieldset` and `legend` for exclusive response and role choices where appropriate.
- Expose selected state programmatically and visibly.
- Keep button labels descriptive: “Copy my action”, not “Copy”.
- Associate every input with a visible label and helper or error text.

### Keyboard and focus

- Every scenario, role choice, habit, edit and export route must work without a mouse.
- Focus order must follow the reading order.
- The visible focus indicator must remain clear on every background.
- Revealing feedback must not move focus unexpectedly.
- If one scenario replaces another, update the progress text and place focus on the new scenario heading only when that behaviour has been tested with assistive technology. A simpler progressive-enhancement pattern is preferable.

### Dynamic feedback

- Use a polite live region for answer feedback, validation, copy and download status.
- Do not rely on colour, icons or position alone to distinguish selected, preferred or error states.
- Keep feedback available long enough to read and revisit.
- Provide a manual recovery route when Clipboard, download or email-app behaviour fails.

### Reflow and cognition

- Test at a 320 CSS-pixel viewport and at 400% desktop zoom without two-dimensional scrolling.
- Test text at 200% and with WCAG text-spacing overrides.
- Keep tap targets at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with 44 by 44 preferred for the primary choices.
- Avoid timers, drag interactions, auto-advancing content and unnecessary animation.
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion` for any smooth scrolling or transitions.
- Ensure core teaching content and the final plain-text plan remain readable if JavaScript fails.

## Evaluation plan

### What to test before a pilot

Use staff from varied roles and levels of digital confidence. Include people who do not work in classrooms and, where feasible, people with relevant access needs.

Ask participants to complete these tasks without coaching:

1. Find and begin Week 3 from the overview.
2. Respond to the “blocked someone” moment and explain why they chose that response.
3. Explain the difference between asking a calm question and investigating.
4. Choose a Week 4 habit for their role.
5. Complete the safeguarding checkpoint.
6. Create, edit and export an action plan using one route.
7. Find what to do if an online-life comment becomes a concern.

Record task success, hesitation, misinterpretation, errors, recovery and time. Do not treat positive comments as proof that the learning works.

### Immediate learning checks

- Can the learner identify the strongest response in a new, unpractised scenario?
- Can they explain why a device inspection or personal investigation is not the default first step?
- Can they name the safeguarding route in their school?
- Is their chosen habit small, role-relevant and tied to a real cue?

### One-week transfer check

Use a short follow-up that asks:

- Did you notice an online-life opening?
- Did you try the habit you chose?
- What made it easier or harder?
- Do you know what you would do if a concern emerged?

Do not ask staff to provide a child’s name or incident details. Report the number of respondents and separate self-reported behaviour from observed behaviour.

### Analytics boundaries

Useful aggregate events may include page start, scenario completion, role-habit activity completion and export method. These show use, not confidence, learning or culture change.

Do not collect scenario answer text, free-text action plans, child information or an individual staff performance score.

## Acceptance criteria

### Learning and content

- Week 3 adds proportionate-response practice rather than repeating Week 2’s rationale.
- Week 4 gives every named staff context at least two realistic habits within that role’s authority.
- Feedback explains why a response helps or closes conversation.
- The safeguarding boundary is explicit, current and approved by the DSL.
- No copy suggests that conversation replaces reporting or that staff should investigate.
- The final action is small, editable and tied to a role or context.

### Interaction

- All five Week 3 moments work with keyboard, pointer and touch.
- The learner can change an answer and revisit feedback.
- Role selection reveals the correct habit set and can be changed without losing control.
- The preferred safeguarding response is shown to every learner.
- Copy, download and email draft routes generate the same plain-text summary.
- Blank action, Clipboard failure and unavailable email behaviour have clear recovery routes.

### Accessibility and privacy

- Heading, landmark, label and control semantics are correct.
- Focus is visible and logical, with no keyboard trap.
- Feedback and errors are announced to assistive technology.
- Contrast, text resize, text spacing and 320 CSS-pixel reflow pass manual checks.
- No activity requires hover, dragging, a timer or precise movement.
- Free text and selected context remain in the browser page only and are not sent to analytics.
- The privacy helper text is visible beside the action and export controls.

### Evidence and release

- The current statutory guidance version is recorded and correctly cited for the release date.
- The DSL has approved the reporting language and scenario feedback.
- Intended staff have completed the critical tasks without moderator rescue.
- High-priority accessibility or safeguarding issues are fixed and re-tested.
- Engagement analytics are not described as evidence of behaviour or culture change.

# Module-specific quality rubric

This rubric assesses whether Weeks 3 and 4 are ready for a controlled staff pilot. It complements the broader `digital-learning-qa-rubric-review-draft.md`, which should still be used for full interface and accessibility QA.

## How to use the rubric

1. Check the five release gates. A failed or untested gate blocks pilot readiness regardless of the score.
2. Score every criterion from 0 to 4.
3. Calculate each contribution as `rating ÷ 4 × weight`.
4. Record the evidence and label it observed, reported, inferred or not tested.
5. Give every criterion below 3 an owner, action and re-test method.

### Rating scale

| Rating | Meaning |
|---:|---|
| 0 | Absent, unsafe or actively contradicts the intended behaviour. |
| 1 | Weak, largely assumption-led and likely to confuse or exclude. |
| 2 | Partly sound but inconsistent, incomplete or weakly evidenced. |
| 3 | Strong, consistently designed or implemented and supported by appropriate review. |
| 4 | Demonstrably effective with intended staff, including varied roles and confidence, after iteration. |

## Release gates

| Gate | Pass requirement | Minimum evidence before pilot |
|---|---|---|
| G1. Safeguarding accuracy | The curiosity, listening, reporting and investigation boundaries are current, child-centred and consistent with the school’s policy. | Dated source check and written DSL approval. |
| G2. Critical accessibility | Every activity and export route can be understood and completed without a mouse or a single sensory cue. | Manual keyboard, focus, screen-reader, contrast, zoom, reflow and text-spacing checks. |
| G3. Privacy and trust | No free text, child information or identifiable staff performance data is transmitted or retained without a clear need and notice. | Data-flow and analytics-event review; browser/network inspection. |
| G4. Functional journey | Week 3 scenarios, Week 4 role habits, checkpoint, editing and all export routes work with clear recovery. | Critical-task and failure-state testing on supported browsers and narrow screens. |
| G5. Evidence honesty | The product does not describe completion, page views or positive comments as proof of safeguarding competence or culture change. | Claims-to-evidence review and pilot reporting template. |

An untested gate is not a pass.

## Decision bands

| Score | Decision | Additional condition |
|---:|---|---|
| 90 to 100 | Ready for wider release consideration | All gates pass with strong evidence; no criterion below 3; behaviour and access claims have representative evidence. |
| 75 to 89 | Ready for a controlled pilot | All gates pass; no safeguarding or critical accessibility criterion below 3; pilot scope and stopping conditions are defined. |
| 60 to 74 | Revise before pilot | The concept may be sound, but a material learning, safeguarding, inclusion or interaction weakness remains. |
| Below 60 | Re-scope | The sequence or critical journey needs redesign before interface polish. |

## Evidence rules

- **Observed:** directly seen in the interface, code, a test or a participant’s behaviour.
- **Reported:** stated by a participant or stakeholder and attributed.
- **Inferred:** a reasoned design judgement that still needs validation.
- **Not tested:** no adequate evidence. Never score this as a pass by default.

Claims that staff understood, felt confident or changed behaviour require staff evidence. Automated accessibility results cannot establish manual accessibility. Page completion cannot establish learning. A DSL review cannot replace usability testing, and usability testing cannot replace the DSL review.
